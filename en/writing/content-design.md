# The job I spent 7 years explaining still doesn't exist.

In December 2024, Lorem UX ran a portrait on me in their "In the office of..." series. Ten questions, clean format, honest answers. Rereading it, I saw the pattern: every answer, even dressed up as anecdote or advice, was saying the same thing. I was trying to explain a job nobody understands. After 7 years, I stopped counting.

## The evangelism tax

A number floats around the UX community: 76% of a UX person's time isn't UX. It's explanation. Convincing teams that your role serves a purpose. I don't know if the number is exact. What I know is it matches what I lived for 7 years.

You're hired to design content. You spend your days justifying why content should be designed. Writing might be 5% of the job. The rest is diplomacy, pedagogy, and organizational survival.

Ben Davies-Romano, Principal Content Designer at Wise, asked the question that sums it all up: "why is the burden of education on the shoulders of professionals who are already overloaded?" Felicia Wu went even more direct: after 10 years of Content Design existing, we've explained enough. If people still don't get it, the problem isn't pedagogy anymore.

## The invisibility paradox

The cruelest irony of the job: the better you do it, the more invisible it becomes. A smooth user flow, zero friction, zero confusion, zero support tickets. The stakeholder looks at the result and congratulates the devs, design, product. The words that guided the user at every step? Nobody sees them.

You can't put "no user got lost" on a slide deck. Your best work looks like blank space. And management doesn't hand out raises for the absence of problems.

Try showing your work at a dinner. A designer pulls up screens. A dev shows their app. A copywriter has their ads. You explain that you replaced "Next" with "Continue" and the drop-off rate went down 3%. People nod politely and change the subject.

## "So you're a copywriter, right?"

The trap of Content Design is the word "writing." The moment people hear it, their brain shortcuts: writing = marketing. You say UX Writer, they hear copywriter. You say Content Designer, they hear copy editor. You spend your life correcting a confusion the title itself causes.

The distinction is simple. A copywriter asks "Who's the target?" and "What's the character limit?". A Content Designer asks "Should this button exist?" and "Is this the right moment in the flow for this interaction?". It's a fundamentally different level of product thinking.

In France it's worse. The job is still emerging: no official classification at France Travail (the public employment service), almost no recognized training. You're not explaining a misunderstood job. You're explaining a job most people have never heard of.

## The title carousel

And as if external confusion wasn't enough, the profession can't even agree on its own name. UX Writer. Content Designer. Product Writer. Content Strategist. UX Content Designer. Each rename is a silent admission that the previous title failed to communicate what we do. And each rename resets the explanation counter to zero.

Filipa Moreno, Senior UX Writer at Santander, summed up the fatigue in February 2025: "I'm tired of the game, the process, the polish requests on screens that are already in development." Ilaya Teejay titled her piece without flinching: "I am tired of being a UX Writer, Content Strategist, and all the roles in between."

When I started in 2019, I was a UX Writer. In 2022, Content Designer. Today, AI Product Builder. Three titles in 5 years, and at every pivot the same question: "So wait, what do you actually do?" That's part of why I left the job. Not out of disinterest. Out of wear. At some point, you get fed up fighting to exist in an org chart that doesn't know where to put you. You'd rather build something where the value is visible, immediate, undeniable. Where nobody asks if your role is actually necessary.

## AI as the third layer of fatigue

For 7 years the question was "What do you do?" Then it became "Why would we pay for that?" And now it's "Can't ChatGPT do it?"

UX job postings dropped 89% between their 2022 peak and early 2024. A third of organizations lost staff. Juniors are getting replaced by tools that generate wireframes, mockups, and copy in minutes. Stakeholders now expect "a person with an infinite skillset."

In the Lorem UX interview, I talked about Microphage-1. A custom GPT I'd built that wrapped all the Content Design guidelines and best practices. My "mini-me," always on. In hindsight, it was already a sign: I was building the tool that made my own role less necessary. A few months later, I became an AI Product Builder. Survival instinct talked before reason did.

## The family dinner test

The worst part is when you step out of tech. Connor Doherty, UX Writer at Wayfair, found the only line that works: "You know when you type your credit card number and you mistype it? I write the message that tells you it's wrong."

Edwin Mohammad, UX Writer at GO-JEK, titled his article without irony: "Are you a UX writer? Nobody knows what you do." Designlab even published a holiday survival guide: "How to explain UX Design to your family during the holidays." The very existence of that article says everything.

My sister can't explain what I do. My mom either. My friends either. At some point, they just stopped asking.

## Not bitter, not nostalgic

I don't regret these 7 years. Content Design taught me everything I know: understand a problem before solving it, think about the person reading before writing a word, measure the impact of every decision. It's the foundation of everything I build today.

But I won't lie: when the Nielsen Norman Group called 2025 a dark period for UX professionals, with widespread pessimism, disillusion, and frustration, I felt relief. Not because people are suffering. Because someone finally said it out loud.

The Lorem UX interview is a snapshot of a specific moment. A job that didn't quite exist in France yet, a guy trying to explain it, and a series of questions that forced me to put words on a discomfort the whole profession shares.

> In 7 years, I changed titles three times. The underlying job stayed the same: understand a problem, find the right words, build something that holds.
